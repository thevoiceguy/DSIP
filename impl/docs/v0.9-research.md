# DSIP v0.9 research

Spec: none (planning document).

Research done 2026-10-04, after v0.8 was finalized (`poc-v0.8.1`). Two tracks:

- **A. Decentralized discovery and identity:** what can make finding someone more decentralized than v0.8's `did:key` + `did:web` + DHT hints.
- **B. A Device Events Profile:** DSIP carrying SNMP traps, syslog alarms and events, working alongside SNMP rather than replacing it.
- **C. Compliance recording:** how an organisation records its own communications when everything is end-to-end encrypted. Added 2026-10-04 at the editor's request.

Repository and package dates were checked against GitHub, crates.io and npm on the research date. **[unverified]** marks a claim no primary source confirmed. Nothing here is decided: section 3 lists the decisions.

---

## 1. Track A — decentralized discovery

### What v0.8 already has

§8.1 puts authority in this order: DID document > alias > cache > hints. The DHT is a hints tier only. Hints are signed, expiring, verified at every hop and by the reader, and selected by §8.3 (§8.5 as rewritten in #74). What remains weak:

- **Availability** under Sybil/eclipse attacks.
- **Lookup privacy.**
- **Bootstrap censorship.**
- **The DID methods.** `did:key` has no document (no endpoints, no rotation). `did:web` trusts DNS and the web host, who can forge the document.

### Findings that matter

- **did:webvh fixes did:web's two weaknesses:**
  - **The host can no longer forge the document.** The document is a hash-chained log of signed entries, and the DID embeds a self-certifying id taken from the first entry. A hostile host can only withhold the log or serve an older version of it.
  - **Rotation is built in**, including pre-rotation, and a DID can move to another domain.

  It carries every document property DSIP reads unchanged: `DSIPSignaling`, `DSIPMailbox`, X25519 `keyAgreement` and `dsipDelegationRevocations`. It slots into §8.1 rule 2 with no change to the authority order.

  Two points to state if adopted: rollback detection (resolver cache, watchers or witnesses), and that its JCS-canonicalized proofs belong to the DID method, outside DSIP's never-canonicalize envelope rule.

  Sources: https://identity.foundation/didwebvh/v1.0/, https://blog.identity.foundation/two-recommended-did-methods/, https://github.com/decentralized-identity/didwebvh-rs.
- **Pkarr would put `did:key` hints on a network vastly larger than a DSIP-only overlay.** The Pkarr address is the same Ed25519 key as the `did:key`, and records are self-signed, so they cannot be forged.
  - Mainline's size: Pkarr's README claims "10+ million nodes" **[unverified]**. Published Sybil measurements date from 2012–13.
  - Three mismatches with today's hints:
    1. A BEP44 record must be signed by the root key, so hints signed by a delegated device cannot be expressed.
    2. A DSIP-JOSE hint exceeds BEP44's 1000-byte limit.
    3. Re-encoding the hint as TXT records breaks the byte-for-byte rule.
  - Workable only as an *additional* carrier, or as a pointer record.

  Sources: https://github.com/pubky/pkarr, https://github.com/pubky/mainline, https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~lw525/MLDHT/.
- **did:dht should not be adopted.** It is the obvious alternative to Pkarr, but it is abandoned and its identity key cannot be rotated. Sources: https://block.xyz/inside/block-contributes-digital-identity-components-to-the-decentralized-identity-foundation, https://github.com/decentralized-identity/did-dht.
- **did:peer is the natural identifier for pairwise contacts** (§19.4 introductions and grants). It is the best option for privacy, but it doesn't help discover a stranger, and DSIP would need its own rotation message. Source: https://identity.foundation/peer-did-method-spec/.
- **Key transparency fits §8.2 aliases.** The `alice@example.com → DID` mapping is the one place a provider could silently swap someone's identity. A transparency log lets the user and outside auditors detect that, and it stays below the DID document in §8.1.

  Sources: https://datatracker.ietf.org/wg/keytrans/documents/, https://engineering.fb.com/2023/04/13/security/whatsapp-key-transparency/, https://security.apple.com/blog/imessage-contact-key-verification/.
- **Nostr's lesson:** publishing a signed "where to find me" record to several channels works. But the first record has to live somewhere well known, and those places concentrate. Measurements of how much each relay actually covers are anecdotal **[unverified]**. Source: https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/65.md.

### Recommendation (track A)

1. **Recommend did:webvh** alongside did:web in §7.2, and eventually instead of it.
2. **Prototype Pkarr/Mainline as an additional carrier for `did:key` hints**, next to the libp2p overlay, not replacing it.
3. **Specify an alias-tier transparency profile aligned with KEYTRANS.** Track the protocol draft rather than freezing on it.
4. Note did:peer for pairwise contacts.
5. Do not adopt did:dht, did:plc or KERI.

## 2. Track B — Device Events Profile, alongside SNMP

### Why SNMP alone falls short for alarms

- **Traps are unconfirmed UDP** (RFC 3416 §4.2.6) and are lost exactly when links fail. There is no sequence number, so nothing detects a gap.
- **Informs are acknowledged per hop only**, and the RFC itself says "no guarantee of delivery" (§4.2.7).
- **v1/v2c security is weak.** The community string travels in cleartext, and the UDP source can be spoofed.
- **v3 USM is rarely used for traps.** The receiver needs a user provisioned for every sender's engine ID (RFC 3414 §1.5.1). The RFC also accepts replay to another receiver that shares the user (§1.5.2).
- **Authentication is hop-scoped in every variant**, and so is syslog over TLS (RFC 5425). Signed syslog (RFC 5848) tried to give end-to-end origin, but deployment is thin **[unverified]**.

Sources: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3416.html, https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3414.html, https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5848.html.

### What DSIP would add, and where it would be redundant

**Adds:**
- an origin signed by an organisation-delegated, revocable key, which survives forwarding across hops and organisations;
- durable delivery across NAT to a mailbox (`accepted` = stored, spec-gap 99);
- an MLS-encrypted on-call group;
- acknowledgement and voice escalation on the same identities, with no SaaS in between;
- alarm streams shared between a NOC, vendors and MSPs without a common tenant.

**Redundant for:** metrics and telemetry, polling, and correlation or root-cause analysis inside a NOC. Leave those to SNMP, YANG-Push, gNMI, Prometheus and OpenNMS.

### Prior art to build on, not reinvent

- **RFC 8632, the YANG alarm model:**
  - An alarm is *state*, keyed by `(resource, alarm-type-id, qualifier)`.
  - Severity is indeterminate, warning, minor, major or critical, with `is-cleared` separate.
  - History keeps a flapping alarm as one alarm.
  - Operator state (`ack`, `closed`, `shelved`) is kept apart from resource state.
  - Publishing an alarm inventory, including `will-clear`, is mandatory.

  https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8632.html
- **RFC 3877, the Alarm MIB.** Its `alarmModelTable` maps a notification OID, plus optionally one varbind's value, to raise or clear, and takes the resource from a varbind. It is the shape the mapping table should follow. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3877.html
- **RFC 3584** maps v1 traps to v2: `snmpTrapOID` and the generic traps. It also defines `snmpTrapCommunity.0`, which must be stripped. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3584.html
- **The common pattern across tools:** a rule table, a dedup key and a clear key, with flapping handled by hold-down at the collector. Examples:
  - OpenNMS `reduction-key` / `clear-key`;
  - Zabbix recovery expressions;
  - Prometheus `for` / `keep_firing_for`;
  - Alertmanager grouping, inhibition and silences;
  - PagerDuty Events v2 `dedup_key` with trigger, acknowledge and resolve.

  Sources: https://docs.opennms.com/horizon/latest/operation/deep-dive/alarms/configuring-alarms.html, https://prometheus.io/docs/alerting/latest/configuration/, https://docs.pagerduty.com/developer/send-alert-event.

### Recommended minimal scope for a first draft

1. **A gateway signs**, with its own delegated key, and the device appears as a *claim* with a rendered evidence basis (`snmpv2c` unauthenticated, `v3-authPriv`, `syslog-tls`). This is the Gateway Profile pattern, G§2 and G§5. Per-device DSIP keys come later.
2. **One `device-event` content object** in the Messaging Profile, carrying:
   - the raw evidence: version, source, `snmpTrapOID`, ordered varbinds, any `snmpTrapAddress`; or the RFC 5424 fields;
   - an optional normalized RFC 8632 alarm: the triple, severity, `is-cleared`, text, times and repeat count.

   It is deposited to the NOC mailbox and posted to the on-call group. The community string is never carried. An unmapped trap is a raw-only notification.
3. **Inputs:** SNMPv1 traps, mapped to v2 per RFC 3584; v2c/v3 traps and informs; RFC 5424 syslog over TLS. An inform is answered only after the mailbox's `accepted`, which turns a hop acknowledgement into an end-to-end "stored" one.
4. **The shape of the mapping table is normative** (RFC 3877-like); vendor content is not.
5. **A dedup/clear state machine** keyed on the triple, scoped by gateway identity, with hold-down at the gateway.
6. **An `alarm-ack` object** (`ack | closed`), deliberately *not* the read receipt: "read" means seen, an ack means "I own it" (RFC 8632). Shelving is deferred.
7. **Escalation:** only the trigger is normative. An unacknowledged alarm at or above a configured severity, past a configured time, leads to a call. The rota and timers are local policy.
8. **A gateway heartbeat**, so the NOC can detect that the gateway went silent.
9. **Out of scope:**
   - acknowledgements flowing back to devices (SNMP SET or NETCONF);
   - YANG-Push and gNMI inputs;
   - correlation;
   - standardized vendor mappings.

**Vectors first:**
- the v1→v2 trap mapping;
- community stripping;
- the dedup/clear state machine (raise, repeat, clear, re-raise);
- `alarm-ack` monotonicity;
- the escalation timer firing, and cancelling on ack.

## 2b. Track C — compliance recording under end-to-end encryption

### The principle

End-to-end encryption protects a conversation from the middle, not from its ends. An organisation is an end of its
staff's communications when it owns their identities, and in DSIP it does: devices are delegated by the
organisation's identity (§7.4) and revocable (§7.5). Compliance recording therefore happens **at endpoints the
organisation controls, and is disclosed**. It never happens by decrypting at relays, by key escrow, or through a
lawful-intercept interface, all of which §3 keeps out of scope and all of which would break the guarantee for every
other party.

### What v0.9 already allows

- **Messaging, through the archive key.** In the default `sync` mode, an identity's history is archived to its
  mailbox, encrypted to the identity's archive key (M§12). An organisation that controls the identity can hold that
  key and read the archive. This needs no change.
- **Messaging, through a recorder device.** The organisation adds a device to the staff member's identity. Through
  the multi-device machinery (M§12.3) it receives every message as a real MLS member. It appears in each group's
  member list, so counterparties can see it, but only as one more device, not as a recorder.
- **Calls, at the endpoint.** The organisation's client records locally and uploads.
- **Calls, through the gateway.** A call that crosses the SIP gateway can be recorded there, because the gateway
  is an endpoint of the DSIP leg (G§2).

### What is missing (v0.9 candidates)

1. **A recording disclosure.** A signed "this session/conversation is recorded" indication:
   - for calls, in the session policy;
   - for messaging, in the conversation or as a content object.

   The other party's client shows it. Some jurisdictions require consent from every party to record, so the
   counterparty must be able to see it and to decline (refuse the call, or leave the conversation). Open questions:
   - whether it is a policy flag or a claim;
   - whether a party's client MUST render it;
   - how it interacts with §18.1 basis rendering.
2. **A recorder device role.** A delegation capability (e.g. `dsip.record`, registry `dsip-delegation-capability`,
   §7.4) marks a device as a compliance recorder. Peers then see "recorded by Acme compliance", not "Bob's third
   device", and the role can be revoked like any delegation. The open question is whether a recorder device may send
   content, or only receive (and receipts).
3. **A recorder leg for calls.** How an endpoint adds a disclosed recording participant to a call: a media fork to
   a recorder the organisation runs, in the manner of SIPREC (RFC 7866) but as a DSIP participant. The recorder holds
   its own delegated identity and receives the media keys like any participant. Open questions:
   - whether the recorder is added by the recording party alone, or needs the session's consent (an `update`);
   - how a recorder leg appears in the WebRTC binding (B§).

Prior art to check before drafting: SIPREC (RFC 7865 metadata, RFC 7866 protocol), and MLS-based messengers'
approaches to enterprise archiving. The status of these is unverified, since this track was not web-researched.
